Where LLMs fall short

Four failure modes, and what to do about each.

Hallucination
IssueInvents fluent, confident answers, fake citations, wrong facts.
FixVerify every fact and source before you use it.
Bias
IssueQuality can be systematically worse for under-represented populations.
FixCheck outputs across groups; don’t assume even coverage.
No accountability
IssueThe model can’t be responsible for an error, you are.
FixKeep a human in the loop to own and sign off the output.
Privacy
IssueText pasted into a cloud tool leaves your machine and may be stored.
FixNever paste PHI. Use a local model, or protect the data first →

Today we focus on the one that’s hardest to undo: data privacy.

Part 5 · Safety & Regulatory

Protecting the data

Three ways to reduce risk, and they are not equally safe.

De-identification
Remove or mask direct identifiers (names, health numbers). Indirect ones, ZIP, age, sex, may remain.
⚠ Re-identification still possible by linkage.
Anonymization
Alter the data so re-identification risk is very low, even combined with other sources. A higher bar.
✓ Much lower risk, harder to achieve well.
Synthetic data
Artificial records that mimic the statistics of the real data, but describe no real person.
✓ No real individuals, if generated carefully.

“De-identified” is not anonymous. A few common fields, ZIP + sex + birth date, can re-identify people.

Reliability, privacy & bias

A practical playbook

Several privacy regulators publish the same step-by-step method for structured data.

•Separates direct identifiers (names, IDs) from indirect ones (postal code, age, sex) that enable linkage.
•Frames risk around an adversary trying to re-identify, and a threshold of acceptable risk.
•Lays out a process: classify variables, set a release model, measure risk, apply controls.
•Key caution: data linkage increases vulnerability. De-identification is a risk level, not a switch.

Ontario's IPC, several EU data protection authorities, and HIPAA's Safe Harbor method all describe versions of this process. When you must share health data, follow a recognized method, don't eyeball it.

Reliability, privacy & bias

Governance, internationally

When AI helps decide about people, several jurisdictions now require you to size up the risk first.

EU · AI Act Four risk tiers, unacceptable to minimal. Diagnosis, triage, and clinical decision support are classed high-risk, with core obligations (conformity assessment, technical documentation, human oversight) applying from August 2026.
Canada · Algorithmic Impact Assessment A public questionnaire that scores an automated decision system across four impact levels and sets the safeguards it needs, more oversight and human sign-off as impact rises.
WHO · Ethics & governance guidance Six principles for AI in health: protect autonomy, promote wellbeing and safety, ensure transparency, foster accountability, ensure inclusiveness and equity, and promote sustainable AI.

None of these were written specifically for research use of chatbots. But the pattern is the same everywhere: the more an AI system's output could change what happens to a person, the more scrutiny it needs, triage, prioritization, and eligibility decisions most of all.

Sources: EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689); Government of Canada, Algorithmic Impact Assessment tool; WHO, Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence for Health, 2021.
